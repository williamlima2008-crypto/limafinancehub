How to Price Small Business Services Profitably

Small Business - Service pricing

WL

Written by William LimaPublished August 22, 2026 · Updated September 23, 2026

Why pricing is more than choosing a number

Pricing is one of the most important financial decisions a service business makes. A price that feels easy to sell may not cover time, overhead, taxes, revisions, admin work and future growth needs.

Profitable pricing starts with clarity. Owners need to know what the service costs to deliver, how much capacity is available and what outcome the customer is paying for.

Calculate the real cost of delivery

Start by listing direct costs and the time required to complete the work. Then add the hidden time that often gets missed.

  • Client calls, proposals and onboarding.
  • Research, preparation and revisions.
  • Project management and follow-up.
  • Software, contractor support and payment fees.

If the price covers only the visible work, the business may be discounting every project without realizing it.

Billable hours are smaller than they look

A full-time year contains about 2,080 working hours. After holidays, vacation and a few sick days, roughly 1,900 remain. But a solo service owner does not bill all of those hours. Sales calls, proposals, bookkeeping, marketing, learning and admin all take time that no client pays for directly.

It is common for independent service providers to bill somewhere between half and two-thirds of their working time. At 55% of 1,900 hours, that is about 1,045 billable hours a year. Pricing as if every working hour were billable is one of the fastest ways to end up busy and underpaid.

To find your own number, track every hour for two or four weeks and label each one billable or non-billable. The result is usually surprising, and it becomes the denominator for every rate calculation you make.

Set a target margin before quoting

A target margin helps you decide whether a quote is sustainable. It does not need to be complicated. Estimate the cost of delivery, add overhead and decide how much profit the business needs for the project to be worth taking.

Different services may need different margins. A highly repeatable service can often be priced differently from custom work that requires more attention and uncertainty.

Worked example: pricing one project

Consider a freelance web designer quoting a small business website. The designer's minimum hourly cost, covering pay, overhead and taxes, is $90.

  1. Estimate all the time. Design and build: 30 hours. Meetings, onboarding and project management: 8 hours. Two rounds of revisions: 4 hours. Total: 42 hours.
  2. Price the time at cost. 42 hours at $90 equals $3,780.
  3. Add direct costs. Premium plugins and stock images: $180. A contract copywriter: $600. Total cost: $4,560.
  4. Apply the target margin. For a 20% profit margin, divide cost by 0.80. $4,560 divided by 0.80 equals $5,700.

The quote becomes $5,700, with $1,140 of profit above the designer's own pay. That profit funds slow months, equipment, training and the next hire.

Markup and margin are not the same

Many owners add 20% to their cost and assume they earned a 20% margin. They did not. A 20% markup on $4,560 gives a price of $5,472, and the profit of $912 is only 16.7% of that price. Margin is always calculated as a share of the price, so use division, not multiplication, when you have a target margin in mind.

Choose the right pricing model

Hourly pricing

Hourly rates are simple, but they can punish efficiency and make revenue harder to predict.

Project pricing

Project pricing works well when scope is clear. It lets customers understand the total commitment upfront.

Retainers

Retainers can stabilize cash flow when clients need ongoing support. Define deliverables carefully so the arrangement stays profitable.

Package services into clear tiers

Packages make pricing easier to understand and easier to sell. Three tiers work well for many service businesses because they give clients a real choice without overwhelming them. A hypothetical bookkeeping practice might offer:

Package Monthly price What is included
Essentials $350 Up to 100 transactions, monthly reconciliation and a P&L report.
Growth $650 Up to 300 transactions, reconciliation, reports and a 30-minute monthly review call.
Plus $1,100 Up to 600 transactions, everything in Growth, plus payroll coordination and quarterly planning.

Each tier has a clear limit, so growth in client volume leads naturally to an upgrade conversation instead of unpaid extra work. The middle tier is often the most popular, so design it to be the one you most want to sell.

Protect scope with plain-language terms

Many pricing problems are actually scope problems. A clear proposal should explain what is included, what is not included, how revisions work and when additional fees apply.

  • Define the deliverables.
  • Set revision limits.
  • Explain payment timing.
  • Clarify rush fees or added work.

Simple boundaries make the customer experience better and protect the business from unpaid labor.

Payment terms are part of your price

Two quotes with the same total can have very different effects on cash flow. A $5,700 project paid 60 days after completion ties up your cash for months, while the same project with a deposit keeps the business funded as you work.

  • Deposits: for project work, a split such as 50% upfront, 25% at a midpoint milestone and 25% at delivery is common.
  • Due dates: shorter terms, such as net 15 instead of net 30, reduce the time you finance your clients.
  • Payment method costs: card processing fees often run around 3%, so decide whether to build them into prices or offer a lower-cost bank transfer option.
  • Late payment terms: state them in the proposal so they are agreed in advance, and check any rules that apply in your state.

For a deeper look at timing, see cash flow planning for small business owners.

Review competitor prices with context

Competitor pricing can be useful, but it should not control your entire strategy. Another business may have different costs, skills, capacity, market position or financial goals.

Use competitor research to understand the market, then price based on your value, delivery model and sustainability.

Signs your prices are too low

Underpricing rarely announces itself. It shows up as a pattern of small warning signs:

  • You are booked two or more months ahead, and nobody ever pushes back on a quote.
  • You work full weeks but cannot afford to hire help or take time off.
  • Your gross margin falls quarter after quarter as costs rise and prices stay the same.
  • You feel resentment toward clients for normal requests, which often signals the price does not match the effort.

If two or more of these sound familiar, test a higher price with the next few new clients before changing existing agreements. Their response gives you real data instead of guesses.

Raise prices with a plan

Price increases are easier when they are connected to value, not panic. Review your prices at least once or twice a year and compare them with costs, demand and capacity.

For existing clients, communicate changes clearly and give reasonable notice. For new clients, update proposals and packages so the new pricing becomes normal in the sales process.

Use pricing as a business health signal

If you are busy but cash is still tight, pricing may be one of the first areas to review. Sustainable prices create room for taxes, savings, better tools and less stressful operations.

Your profit and loss statement can show whether price changes are actually improving gross profit and net income over time.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate a minimum hourly rate for my service business?

Add your target annual pay, business overhead and a tax set-aside, then divide by realistic billable hours rather than total working hours. For example, $60,000 in pay plus $12,000 in overhead plus $18,000 for taxes equals $90,000, and dividing by 1,000 billable hours gives a floor of $90 per hour. Pricing below that floor loses money even when you are busy.

How should I charge for revisions or extra work?

Include a defined number of revision rounds in the base price, often one or two, and bill additional rounds at your hourly rate or a fixed fee stated in the proposal. Putting the rule in writing before work starts keeps the conversation simple when a request goes beyond scope.

Should I publish my prices on my website?

Publishing starting prices or package ranges can filter out poor-fit leads and shorten sales calls, especially for standardized services. For custom work, a line such as "projects typically start at" gives guidance without committing to a number before the scope is clear.

How much notice should I give clients before a price increase?

Thirty to sixty days is a common courtesy for ongoing clients, and retainer clients may need notice before their renewal date. Explain the effective date, the new rate and what is included, and apply the new price to new clients right away.

What is a healthy profit margin for a service business?

It varies widely by industry and business model, so there is no single correct number. Track the gross margin of each project, meaning revenue minus direct labor and contractor costs, and compare it across clients and services to see which work actually supports the business.
