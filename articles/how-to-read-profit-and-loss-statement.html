How to Read a Profit and Loss Statement for Your Small Business

Small Business - P&L statement basics

What a profit and loss statement shows

A profit and loss statement, often called a P&L statement or income statement, summarizes revenue, expenses and profit over a period of time. For a small business, that period might be one month, one quarter or one year.

The basic idea is straightforward: revenue minus expenses equals net income or loss. The value comes from the detail between those lines. A good P&L can show whether sales are growing, margins are shrinking, overhead is creeping up or a profitable month only looked good because one unusual expense had not arrived yet.

Learning how to read a profit and loss statement helps owners move beyond bank balance guessing. Cash in the account matters, but it does not always explain whether the business model is healthy.

The main sections of a P&L statement

Formats vary, but most small business income statements follow a similar path from top to bottom.

Section What it means Owner question
Revenue Sales or income earned during the period. Where did money come from?
Cost of goods sold Direct costs tied to delivering products or services. What did it cost to produce the sale?
Gross profit Revenue minus direct costs. Is the core offer priced sustainably?
Operating expenses Overhead such as rent, software, marketing and admin costs. What does it cost to run the business?
Net income Profit or loss after expenses. Did the business make money for the period?

Start at the top: revenue

Revenue shows what the business earned from sales, services, fees or other operating income. Do not stop at the total. Look for the sources behind it.

If one client represents most of the revenue, the business may have concentration risk. If revenue increased because of a one-time project, do not assume the new level will repeat. If sales are growing but cash feels tight, compare the P&L with your invoice timing and cash flow forecast.

A strong revenue line is encouraging, but it is only the first chapter. The next question is how much of that revenue remains after direct costs.

Understand gross profit and gross margin

Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold, or COGS. For a product business, COGS may include inventory, materials, packaging and direct labor. For a service business, it may include contractors, platform fees or direct delivery costs.

Gross margin turns gross profit into a percentage. If revenue is $20,000 and direct costs are $8,000, gross profit is $12,000. Gross margin is 60% because $12,000 is 60% of $20,000.

This matters because overhead still has to be paid from gross profit. A business with low gross margin may need high volume to survive. A service business with strong gross margin may still struggle if operating expenses are too high or owner pay is not planned.

Review operating expenses carefully

Operating expenses are the costs of running the business that are not directly tied to one sale. Common examples include rent, utilities, software, insurance, marketing, bookkeeping, education, payroll support and professional fees.

Look for trends, not just single numbers. A marketing expense may be worthwhile if it supports profitable sales. A software subscription may be essential if it saves hours. But small recurring costs can pile up quietly and reduce profit month after month.

If expenses are hard to classify, revisit your bookkeeping categories and the way you separate business and personal finances. Cleaner records make the P&L easier to trust.

Read net income with context

Net income is the bottom line, but it should not be read in isolation. A profitable month can still create cash strain if customers have not paid yet. A low-profit month may be acceptable if it included a planned annual expense or investment in future capacity.

Compare net income with your cash flow plan. The P&L answers whether the business earned profit during the period. Cash flow answers whether money arrived and left at the right time to keep operations stable.

For that timing view, pair your P&L review with cash flow planning for small business owners.

Example: reading a simple P&L

Imagine a small design studio with $18,000 in monthly revenue. It paid $4,000 to contractors and $1,000 in payment processing and direct project costs. Gross profit is $13,000.

The studio then paid $2,000 for software and tools, $1,500 for marketing, $1,000 for insurance and professional fees, and $4,500 for owner pay. Operating expenses total $9,000, leaving $4,000 in net income before taxes and any other items.

That result may look healthy, but the owner should still ask useful questions: Was revenue from repeatable work? Did contractor costs rise? Is owner pay realistic? Is enough being set aside for taxes? Would a slow month still cover fixed expenses?

Red flags to watch for

  • Revenue is rising but net income is flat or falling.
  • Gross margin changes sharply without a clear reason.
  • Personal expenses appear in business categories.
  • Large "miscellaneous" categories hide important detail.
  • Owner pay is missing, irregular or not shown clearly.
  • Taxes are ignored until cash gets tight.

None of these automatically means the business is in trouble. They are prompts for better questions and cleaner follow-up.

How often should owners review the P&L?

A monthly review is useful for most small businesses. It gives you time to spot problems before they become annual surprises. Quarterly reviews help with bigger decisions, such as pricing, hiring, debt, equipment and owner compensation.

Use the same rhythm each time: compare the current period with the previous period, compare actual results with your plan and write down two or three actions. A report only becomes useful when it changes what you do next.

Cash basis vs accrual basis

Your P&L may be prepared on a cash basis or an accrual basis. Cash basis accounting generally records income when money is received and expenses when money is paid. Accrual basis accounting generally records income when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, even if cash moves later.

This difference can change how the report looks. A cash-basis P&L may look weak in a month when customers paid late, even if work was completed. An accrual-basis P&L may show revenue before the cash is in the bank. Neither view is automatically better for every owner. The important part is knowing which view you are reading.

Questions to ask during every review

A P&L review is easier when you use the same questions each month. Start with revenue: did sales come from repeatable sources, or from one-time work? Then review gross profit: did direct costs rise faster than revenue? Next, review operating expenses: which costs increased, and were they intentional?

Finally, connect the report to action. Do prices need to change? Should a subscription be canceled? Is a contractor arrangement still profitable? Do you need a larger cash reserve before hiring? The report is not there to judge the owner. It is there to make the next decision less vague.

When the P&L looks profitable but cash is tight

This is one of the most confusing small business moments. It can happen when invoices are unpaid, inventory was purchased before sales, debt payments are large, taxes were set aside separately or the owner took draws that are not shown as operating expenses.

When profit and cash do not seem to match, review accounts receivable, accounts payable, debt payments, owner withdrawals and tax reserves. The P&L is one important report, but it works best alongside your balance sheet and cash flow plan.

It can also help to set a separate small business cash reserve so a profitable but slow-paying month does not create immediate operating pressure.

Useful ratios for a simple P&L review

You do not need advanced finance training to use a few basic percentages. Gross margin shows how much of each sales dollar remains after direct costs. Operating expense ratio compares overhead with revenue. Net profit margin shows how much revenue remains as profit after expenses.

For example, if monthly revenue is $30,000 and net income is $3,000, net profit margin is 10%. The number is most useful when compared over time. If margin was 15% last quarter and 10% this quarter, investigate whether prices, direct costs, discounts or overhead changed.

Keep the report readable

A P&L with too many categories can be just as confusing as one with too few. Use enough detail to make decisions, but avoid turning every small purchase into its own line. Group similar expenses in a way that reflects how you manage the business.

For example, separate advertising from software if those categories drive different decisions. But you may not need separate lines for every small office supply purchase. Readability matters because the report should become part of the owner's routine, not a document opened only at tax time.
