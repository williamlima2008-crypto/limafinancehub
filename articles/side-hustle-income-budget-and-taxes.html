Side Hustle Income: How to Budget and Handle Taxes as a Freelancer

Personal Finance - Freelance and side hustle money

WL

Written by William LimaPublished September 23, 2026

Why side income needs its own system

Freelance design work, rideshare driving, tutoring, selling crafts, pet sitting and consulting all have something in common: nobody withholds taxes from the payments. A $500 payment from a client feels like $500 of spendable money, but part of it already belongs to the IRS and possibly your state.

Side income is also irregular. One month brings $1,800; the next brings $200. Without a system, it tends to disappear into everyday spending, and the tax bill arrives months later with nothing set aside. The good news is that the system can be simple: separate the money, set aside a percentage for taxes, track expenses and give the remainder a specific job.

Step 1: Give side income its own account

Open a separate checking account, even a free one, and have every side payment land there. Pay side-business expenses from the same account. This one change makes it easy to see what you actually earned, simplifies tax preparation and keeps personal spending from mixing with business records.

Many people also open a second savings account labeled "Taxes" and move a set percentage into it each time a payment arrives. Money in that account is not yours to spend. If your side work grows into a real business, the habits in our guide to separating business and personal finances become even more important.

Step 2: Understand the taxes you owe

All income is generally taxable, whether or not you receive a Form 1099 from a client or platform. If you work for yourself, you typically owe two kinds of federal tax on your profit:

  • Self-employment tax: this is Social Security and Medicare for self-employed people. Employees split these taxes with their employers, but self-employed people pay both halves, a combined 15.3%, on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings. It generally applies once net earnings from self-employment reach $400 for the year.
  • Income tax: side profit is added to your other income, such as a W-2 salary, and taxed at your marginal bracket. You can deduct half of your self-employment tax when calculating income tax.

Most states with an income tax will also tax side income. Your profit is what you earned minus deductible business expenses, which is why tracking expenses matters as much as tracking income.

How much to set aside: a worked example

Suppose you have a full-time job that places you in the 22% federal bracket, and your side work produces $12,000 of profit for the year.

  • Self-employment tax: $12,000 × 92.35% × 15.3% is about $1,696.
  • Half of that, about $848, is deductible, leaving roughly $11,152 of side income subject to income tax.
  • Federal income tax at 22%: about $2,453.
  • Total estimated federal tax: about $4,149, or roughly 35% of the side profit.

Some side businesses also qualify for the qualified business income deduction, which can lower the income tax portion, and your state may add more. That is why many freelancers set aside 25% to 35% of every payment, adjusting after their first year of real numbers. Setting aside too much is an easy problem to fix at tax time. Setting aside too little is not.

Step 3: Pay estimated taxes on time

Because no one withholds tax from side income, the IRS generally expects you to pay during the year through quarterly estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more when you file. The usual due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15 of the following year, shifting when a date falls on a weekend or holiday. You can pay online through IRS Direct Pay or your IRS online account.

To avoid an underpayment penalty, a common safe harbor is paying, through withholding and estimated payments combined, at least 100% of last year's total tax, or 110% if your adjusted gross income was above $150,000, or at least 90% of this year's tax.

A shortcut for people with a regular job

If you also have a W-2 job, you can cover side-income taxes by increasing withholding at that job instead of sending quarterly payments. Enter an extra amount per paycheck in Step 4(c) of your Form W-4. For the example above, spreading about $4,150 across 26 paychecks means roughly $160 of extra withholding per paycheck. Withholding is treated as paid evenly during the year, which can help avoid penalties even if your side income is lumpy. Our guide to reading your paycheck and the W-4 walks through the form.

Step 4: Track deductible expenses

Business expenses that are ordinary and necessary for your work generally reduce your taxable profit. Common examples for side hustles include:

  • Platform, marketplace and payment processing fees.
  • Software, website hosting and subscriptions used for the work.
  • Supplies, materials and equipment.
  • Business mileage, tracked with a log, or actual vehicle expenses for business use.
  • The business-use share of your phone and internet.
  • A home office, if a specific area is used regularly and exclusively for the business.

Keep receipts and a simple log. A spreadsheet with date, vendor, amount, category and purpose is enough for most side hustles. Record expenses weekly rather than trying to reconstruct a year of receipts in April.

Step 5: Budget the rest with a percentage split

Once taxes and expenses have a place, split the remainder by rule so every payment is handled the same way. A simple starting split:

Bucket Share of each payment On an $800 payment
Taxes 30% $240
Business expenses and reinvestment 10% $80
Primary goal (debt, emergency fund or savings target) 45% $360
Guilt-free spending 15% $120

If you rely on side income for essential bills, build your household budget on your regular income alone and treat side income as extra. That way, a slow month does not put rent at risk. The approach mirrors the conservative baseline used in a debt payoff plan for variable income.

Watch for tax forms, but do not depend on them

Clients may send Form 1099-NEC, and payment platforms may send Form 1099-K, but the reporting thresholds for these forms have changed several times in recent years, and many small amounts will not generate a form at all. Your own records, not the forms, should be the source of truth. At year end, compare any forms you receive with your income log and investigate differences.

Save for retirement on side income

Self-employment income opens retirement options beyond your workplace plan. A SEP IRA or a solo 401(k) can let you contribute part of your side profit to a tax-advantaged account, which also lowers your current tax bill if you use traditional contributions. Rules and limits depend on your net earnings and other plans, so read the IRS guidance or ask a tax professional before choosing.

A monthly side hustle routine

Once a month, total income and expenses, confirm the tax account holds your set-aside percentage, log mileage and receipts and move goal money where it belongs. Before each quarterly due date, compare year-to-date profit with your estimate and adjust the payment. The routine takes about 20 minutes, and it turns April from a scramble into a formality.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to pay taxes on side income if I did not receive a 1099?

Yes. Income is generally taxable whether or not a client or platform sends a form. Self-employment tax generally applies once your net earnings from self-employment reach $400 for the year, so keep your own records of every payment.

Do I need an LLC for my side hustle?

No. You can operate as a sole proprietor using your own name and Social Security number or an EIN. An LLC can offer liability protection and may make sense as the business grows, but it does not by itself lower your taxes.

What happens if I miss a quarterly estimated tax payment?

The IRS may charge an underpayment penalty, which works much like interest on the amount that was paid late. Paying as soon as possible limits the penalty, and increasing your W-2 withholding for the rest of the year can also help.

Can I deduct my home internet bill?

You can generally deduct the business-use portion. If you use your internet for side work about 30% of the time, about 30% of the cost may be deductible. Keep a reasonable record of how you estimated the percentage.

What is the difference between a hobby and a side business?

A business is carried on with the intention of making a profit, while a hobby is mainly for enjoyment. The IRS looks at factors such as how you run the activity, the time you spend and whether you depend on the income. Hobby income is still taxable, but hobby expenses generally cannot be deducted.
