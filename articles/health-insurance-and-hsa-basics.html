Health Costs

Health Insurance and HSA Basics for Your Household Budget

Saving & Budgeting - Health insurance and HSAs

WL

Written by William LimaPublished September 23, 2026

Why health costs belong in your budget plan

Health insurance is one of the largest and least predictable costs in many U.S. household budgets. The monthly premium is easy to see because it comes out of every paycheck. The rest, including deductibles, copays and coinsurance, arrives unevenly, often at the worst possible time.

A budget that includes only the premium is missing part of the picture. The goal of this guide is to help you understand how the pieces fit together, compare plans by what they are likely to cost over a full year and decide whether a health savings account (HSA) can make those costs easier to manage.

The five terms that drive your costs

Term What it means
Premium What you pay every month to keep coverage, whether or not you use care.
Deductible The amount you pay for covered services each year before the plan starts sharing costs. Many preventive services are covered before the deductible.
Copay A fixed fee for a service, such as $30 for a primary care visit.
Coinsurance Your percentage of the cost after the deductible, such as 20%.
Out-of-pocket maximum The most you pay for covered in-network care in a plan year. After you reach it, the plan pays 100% of covered costs. Premiums do not count toward it.

How the pieces work together: an example

Imagine a plan with a $2,000 deductible, 20% coinsurance and a $5,000 out-of-pocket maximum. In March, you need an outpatient procedure with an allowed cost of $8,000.

  • You pay the first $2,000 to meet the deductible.
  • The remaining $6,000 is shared: you pay 20%, or $1,200, and the plan pays $4,800.
  • Your total cost for the procedure is $3,200.

If you need more care later in the same plan year, you would pay coinsurance until your total reaches $5,000. After that, covered in-network care costs you nothing more for the rest of the year. This is why the out-of-pocket maximum is the most important number for worst-case budgeting: it tells you the ceiling on your medical bills, apart from premiums and out-of-network care.

Common plan types

HMO

Health maintenance organizations usually require you to use in-network providers and get referrals from a primary care doctor to see specialists. Premiums are often lower.

PPO

Preferred provider organizations offer more flexibility to see specialists and out-of-network providers, usually at a higher premium.

EPO

Exclusive provider organizations typically cover only in-network care, like an HMO, but often without referral requirements.

High-deductible health plan (HDHP)

An HDHP has a higher deductible and usually a lower premium. If it meets IRS requirements, it makes you eligible to contribute to an HSA.

What an HSA is and why it is unusual

A health savings account is a tax-advantaged account for medical expenses, available to people covered by an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan who have no other disqualifying coverage, are not enrolled in Medicare and cannot be claimed as someone else's dependent. Beginning in 2026, federal law also treats many bronze and catastrophic marketplace plans as HSA-compatible, so check your plan documents for eligibility.

HSAs offer a rare triple tax advantage:

  • Contributions are tax-deductible, or pre-tax when made through payroll.
  • Growth, including interest and investment gains, is not taxed.
  • Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.

For 2026, the IRS contribution limit is $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, with an extra $1,000 allowed for people 55 and older. Employer contributions count toward the limit.

The money is yours. Unused balances roll over every year, and the account stays with you if you change jobs or plans. Many HSAs allow you to invest the balance once it exceeds a minimum, which lets some people use the account as a long-term health fund for retirement. Withdrawals for non-medical expenses before age 65 are taxed and generally face a 20% penalty; after 65, they are taxed like traditional retirement account withdrawals.

Keep receipts: the HSA reimbursement strategy

HSA withdrawals do not have to happen in the same year as the medical expense. As long as the expense occurred after you opened the HSA, you can generally reimburse yourself later, even years later, if you keep documentation. Some people pay small medical bills from their regular budget, save the receipts in a digital folder and leave the HSA invested to grow. The receipts become a tax-free withdrawal option they can use whenever they need cash.

This approach only works if your regular budget can absorb the bills and you keep careful records. If paying out of pocket would strain your cash flow, using the HSA directly is the better choice.

HSA vs FSA

Feature HSA Health FSA
Eligibility Requires an HSA-eligible plan. Offered by employers, regardless of plan type.
Ownership You own it and keep it when you leave a job. Tied to your employer.
Unused money Rolls over indefinitely. Mostly use-it-or-lose-it, with a limited carryover or grace period if the plan allows.
Investing Often available. Not available.

Comparing plans by total annual cost

The cheapest premium is not always the cheapest plan. Compare plans by estimating total annual cost in a low-use year and a high-use year. Here is a hypothetical comparison for single coverage through an employer:

Item Plan A: PPO Plan B: HDHP with HSA
Annual premiums $2,160 ($180 per month) $960 ($80 per month)
Deductible / out-of-pocket maximum $1,000 / $4,000 $3,000 / $6,000
Employer HSA contribution None $750
Low-use year ($500 of care) $2,660 $710
Worst-case year (maximum reached) $6,160 $6,210

In this example, the HDHP saves about $1,950 in a low-use year and costs only $50 more in a worst-case year, before counting the tax savings on your own HSA contributions. The PPO may still be the better fit if you have ongoing prescriptions, planned surgery or a strong preference for particular doctors, but the numbers should drive the decision rather than the premium alone. Always confirm that your doctors and prescriptions are in network before choosing.

Building health costs into the monthly budget

Once you have chosen a plan, treat expected out-of-pocket costs like any other irregular expense. Estimate what you spent on copays, prescriptions and deductibles last year, then divide by 12. If you expect $1,200 in out-of-pocket costs, a $100 monthly transfer to an HSA or a medical sinking fund keeps those bills from landing on a credit card.

If you have an HDHP, a strong target is to build your HSA balance to at least your deductible. That way, a surprise diagnosis becomes a withdrawal rather than a crisis. Your general emergency fund then covers income disruptions and other shocks.

Ways to lower medical bills

  • Use preventive care, which many plans cover at no cost when you see in-network providers.
  • Ask whether a lab, imaging center or pharmacy has a lower price than the hospital or default option.
  • Request an itemized bill and compare it with your plan's explanation of benefits before paying.
  • Ask the provider about payment plans or financial assistance for large bills. Many hospitals have assistance programs.

An open enrollment checklist

Open enrollment is the one time each year when most people can change plans. Use it deliberately: review last year's medical spending, check networks for your doctors, compare total annual cost for each plan, confirm HSA eligibility and employer contributions and update your payroll contributions for the new year.

Frequently asked questions

What happens to my HSA if I change jobs?

The HSA belongs to you, so the balance stays yours when you leave a job. You can keep it where it is or move it to another HSA provider. You can only make new contributions for months when you are covered by an HSA-eligible plan.

Can I use my HSA for dental and vision expenses?

Yes. Qualified medical expenses generally include dental care such as cleanings and fillings and vision care such as eye exams, glasses and contact lenses. Purely cosmetic procedures usually do not qualify, and IRS Publication 502 lists what counts.

Can I contribute to an HSA if my spouse has a health FSA?

Often no. If your spouse has a general-purpose health FSA that can reimburse your medical expenses, that coverage usually makes you ineligible for HSA contributions. A limited-purpose FSA for dental and vision is generally compatible.

Do premiums count toward my out-of-pocket maximum?

No. The out-of-pocket maximum includes deductibles, copays and coinsurance for covered in-network care, but premiums are paid separately. When budgeting for a worst-case year, add annual premiums to the out-of-pocket maximum.

Should I invest my HSA balance?

Many people keep enough cash in the HSA to cover their deductible and invest anything above that for long-term growth. Money you may need for medical bills in the near future should stay in cash, because investments can lose value.
